Post-Christianity and the Dawn of What

Western religious decline isn't a prediction or a culture-war talking point, it's a fifty-year trend line with consistent numbers behind it. What's less often shown is what actually happens on the other side: not a clean switch to atheism, but a slow separation of belonging from believing, and a decline that is, so far, almost entirely a Western phenomenon rather than a global one.

Growth accelerated sharply in the 1990s and has slowed somewhat since 2019, but the direction hasn't reversed in fifty years of measurement. Every age group, education level, and region shows the same trend line, up.

Believing Without Belonging: Scandinavia

Sweden and Denmark show where the American trend may eventually head, and it isn't straightforward atheism. Formal church membership stays surprisingly high while practice and literal belief collapse almost entirely, the two separate rather than move together.

Membership in the Church of Denmark stands at 70% as of 2026. People keep the formal, often inherited, affiliation even as practice disappears underneath it.

Only 3% of Swedes and 7% of Danes attend church weekly, among the lowest rates in the world. About a third of Swedes say they believe in some kind of god or spiritual force at all, well under half.

Sociologist Grace Davie's phrase for the broader pattern is "believing without belonging" in most Western contexts; Scandinavia runs it in reverse, belonging without believing, institutional membership persisting as cultural inheritance long after the theology underneath it has quietly emptied out.

The Part That's Easy to Miss

Secularization Is a Western Story, Not a Global One

As of 2020, roughly 84% of the world's population identifies with a religious group, and that share had barely moved in the prior decade. The countries driving religious growth globally, concentrated in sub-Saharan Africa and parts of Asia, have significantly higher fertility rates than the secularizing West.

The plain implication: even as Sweden, Denmark, and increasingly the United States drift toward unaffiliation, the global religious population is still growing in absolute terms, and may continue to for decades. What looks like the end of religion from inside a Western living room looks like relocation from a wider vantage point, not disappearance.

None of this predicts where any individual reader lands. Trend lines describe populations, not people. What the data does support is a specific, falsifiable claim: institutional religious affiliation in the West has been declining consistently for fifty years, the decline outpaces any reversal on record, and the story most often told about what replaces it, mass atheism, undersells how much of it is actually a quiet uncoupling of identity from belief rather than a clean conversion to unbelief.
