The Case Against

A Critical Reference on the Bible's Origins, Authorship, and Claims

In 1998, Lee Strobel published The Case for Christ — a former journalist's structured argument for the historical reliability of the Gospels, presenting witness after witness for the defense. This is the other side of that courtroom.

What follows is organized the way a prosecuting attorney builds a case: establishing what the document actually is before examining what it borrowed, what it got wrong, and what it has historically concealed. Each section calls a different witness — archaeologist, textual critic, historian, linguist — and each witness says something the pew never hears.

The Methodological Starting Point

The Bible is treated here as what it demonstrably is: a library of texts produced over roughly a millennium by many hands, in multiple languages, compiled under political and theological pressure. That is not a taunt — it is the methodological starting point of modern historical criticism, practiced by scholars at seminaries, divinity schools, and secular universities alike. It is what allows the texts to become explainable rather than merely quoted.

What This Is Not

This is not an argument that religious experience is invalid, or that meaning cannot be found in ancient texts. It is an argument about historical claims specifically — authorship, dating, archaeology, prophecy, and textual integrity. Where the evidence is strong, no false balance is offered. Where scholarly opinion is genuinely divided, that is flagged. The goal is accuracy, not polemics.

The Roadmap

Fifteen sections organized into four acts. Read straight through or jump directly to any section. The argument builds — but every section also stands on its own.

Act I The Document Itself

Before examining what the Bible claims, we examine what it actually is: who wrote it, when, under what circumstances, and what the texts reveal about their own composite origins. This is where the defense's first premise — that the Bible is a unified, reliably transmitted, historically accurate document — begins to come apart.

Section I
Internal Contradictions
The Bible contradicts itself — on who killed Goliath, how Judas died, what happened at the empty tomb, how many animals Noah took, and whether God changes his mind. These are not peripheral puzzles. They are seams where independent source documents were stitched together, still visible to any careful reader.
→ Section II
Authorship Questions
Moses did not write the Torah. Isaiah did not write all of Isaiah. The Gospels were written by anonymous Greeks, not eyewitness disciples. The Pastoral Epistles were written decades after Paul's death in Paul's name. This section catalogs what critical scholars — including those at Catholic and Protestant seminaries — actually conclude about who wrote each major book.
→ Section III
The Four Sources (JEDP)
The Torah is not a single document but a composite of four source traditions — the Yahwist, the Elohist, the Deuteronomist, and the Priestly source — each written by a different faction, in a different century, with a distinct political and theological agenda. This section identifies each source, its fingerprints, and its motivations.
→ Section IV
The Flood: A Case Study
The Flood narrative in Genesis is the textbook demonstration of the Documentary Hypothesis. Separated by divine name and vocabulary, two complete, internally consistent, and mutually contradictory flood myths emerge — each with its own theology, its own animal count, its own duration, and its own portrait of God. They were never reconciled because the editor's job was preservation, not harmonization.
→ Section V
Anachronisms & the Patriarchs
Abraham's camels did not exist in his era. The Philistines he interacts with hadn't arrived yet. "Ur of the Chaldeans" references a people who didn't exist until centuries later. The city of Dan wasn't called Dan. Each anachronism is a timestamp proving these narratives were written not in the Bronze Age but in the Iron Age — and reflect the world of their authors, not their subjects.
→ Section VI
Moses & the Exodus
There is no Egyptian record of a mass Israelite enslavement, no archaeological trace of a forty-year wilderness sojourn for two million people, and no evidence of a large-scale migration into Canaan from Egypt. The numbers in the text itself are impossible. What leading archaeologists — including Israel Finkelstein and William Dever — actually conclude about the origins of the Mosaic tradition.

Act II Where It Came From

The Bible did not emerge from a vacuum. Its stories, rituals, and theology were shaped by — and in many cases borrowed directly from — the older cultures surrounding ancient Israel. This act traces those origins, from Mesopotamian flood myths to Egyptian wisdom literature to the Canaanite gods Israel's own ancestors worshipped.

Section VII
Borrowed Mythology
The Flood story predates the Bible by centuries in the Epic of Gilgamesh and the Atrahasis Epic. Moses's basket-in-the-river birth mirrors the birth legend of Sargon of Akkad. Proverbs 22–23 is a near-direct translation of the Egyptian Instruction of Amenemope. Psalm 104 parallels the Hymn to the Aten. These are not vague thematic similarities — they are specific, sequenced, textual parallels with documented Mesopotamian and Egyptian sources.
→ Section VIII
Polytheistic Origins of Judaism
Yahweh had a consort named Asherah — we know because 8th-century BCE inscriptions say so. The Dead Sea Scrolls preserve an older text of Deuteronomy 32 in which the Most High divides the nations among "the sons of God," with Yahweh receiving Israel as his portion. Psalm 82 shows God presiding over a council of other gods. Strict monotheism was a late development, and what the prophets called "idolatry" was Israel's own ancestral Canaanite religion.
→ Section IX
Formation of the Canon
The Bible was assembled over centuries by human beings making contested decisions. Marcion of Sinope — a heretic — inadvertently forced the proto-orthodox church to define its canon. The Council of Nicaea did not vote on which books to include (a persistent myth). The 27-book New Testament is first listed in a bishop's Easter letter of 367 CE. The deuterocanonical books were added and removed based on theological disputes about purgatory.

Act III What It Got Wrong

If the Bible is divinely inspired and inerrant, its specific historical and prophetic claims should be verifiable. This act examines the record: prophecies that failed by the authors' own admission, manuscripts that were demonstrably altered, a central doctrine built on a mistranslation, and the remarkable thinness of the evidence for the Bible's central historical figure.

Section X
Failed Prophecies
Ezekiel predicted Nebuchadnezzar would destroy Tyre. He didn't — and Ezekiel acknowledged the failure in a later oracle, offering Egypt as a make-good. Daniel's end-times timeline was about Antiochus IV Epiphanes in 165 BCE, not a distant future — and the prediction failed when Antiochus died in Persia rather than Israel as predicted. Yahweh promised David an eternal throne. It ended in 586 BCE and has never been restored.
→ Section XI
Textual Corruption
The woman caught in adultery (John 8) is not in the oldest manuscripts. The resurrection appearances in Mark (16:9–20) were added by a later scribe to a Gospel that originally ended with an empty tomb and frightened women. The explicit Trinitarian proof-text (1 John 5:7) appears in no Greek manuscript before the 14th century — it is a Latin interpolation that Erasmus refused to include until pressured. These are not fringe claims; they appear in the footnotes of every modern study Bible.
→ Section XII
The Historical Jesus Problem
Not a single contemporary source mentions Jesus. Josephus's famous passage is at least partly a Christian forgery. Tacitus, writing 80 years later, reports what Christians of his own day believed — not what an archive confirmed. The mythicist position (that Jesus began as a celestial figure, not a historical man) is a serious minority view held by credentialed scholars, though the mainstream disagrees. What we can say with genuine historical confidence is remarkably sparse.
→ Section XIII
The Virgin Birth Problem
The doctrine rests on a Greek mistranslation of a Hebrew word. Isaiah 7:14 uses almah (young woman); the Septuagint rendered it as parthenos (virgin); Matthew built his birth narrative on the Greek. The original passage is explicitly about the 8th-century BCE Syro-Ephraimite war — a sign to King Ahaz, not a prediction seven centuries forward. The two Gospel birth narratives are also irreconcilable with each other, and Paul, Mark, and John never mention a virgin birth at all.
→
Act IV What It Conceals

The final act examines not what the Bible got factually wrong but what it endorses that its modern defenders would rather not discuss — and what the apologetic responses to those endorsements reveal about the nature of the text itself. Every defense of the conquest narratives and the slavery passages ultimately concedes that the Bible reflects the moral horizon of the humans who wrote it.

Section XIV
Divine Violence
God commands the complete extermination of the Canaanites, including infants. Saul is rejected as king for failing to complete the genocide of the Amalekites. The Midianite campaign ends with Moses ordering the killing of every non-virgin woman and girl. The best evangelical defense of these passages — Paul Copan's Is God a Moral Monster? — ultimately appeals to cultural convention and archaeological uncertainty to soften them. That appeal concedes exactly what it is trying to deny.
→ Section XV
Slavery & the Bible
Exodus 21 specifies that a slave beaten to death slowly is not a homicide — "for the slave is his money." Leviticus 25 licenses the permanent purchase of foreign slaves as heritable property. The New Testament instructs slaves to obey their masters. American slaveholders cited these texts accurately. The abolitionist movement ultimately succeeded not because of scripture but despite it — driven by Enlightenment arguments about human dignity that owe nothing to Leviticus. The "cultural context" apologetic concedes the very point it means to refute.
