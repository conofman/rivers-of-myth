Deep Dive

Hell's Family Tree

English Bibles use one word, hell, to translate at least four different underlying words from three different languages and cultures, each with its own separate meaning, none of which originally described a fiery place of eternal conscious torment. The modern concept is a composite, assembled over roughly two thousand years out of Hebrew burial language, Persian dualism, Greek mythology, apocalyptic literature, patristic argument, and one fourteenth-century Italian poem that did more to shape the popular image than any verse of scripture.

Four Words, One English Word

Every one of these gets translated "hell" in most English Bibles. They are not synonyms, and conflating them is where most of the popular confusion starts.

Sheol
Hebrew · Hebrew Bible, 66 occurrences

The common destination of everyone who dies, righteous and wicked alike, described as a shadowy underground realm of silence and dust. Ecclesiastes states plainly that the same fate comes to all.

There is no fire, no torment, and no moral sorting in any Old Testament description of Sheol. Scholarly consensus, including critical scholars like Bart Ehrman, is unanimous that Sheol never means "hell" in the modern punitive sense.

Verdict: not originally a place of punishment at all
Hades
Greek · adopted into Jewish and Christian usage

Originally the Greek god and realm of the dead generally, not specifically a place of punishment. When the Hebrew Bible was translated into Greek (the Septuagint, c. 200s BCE), translators substituted "Hades" for "Sheol" simply because the concepts seemed similar enough.

The New Testament inherits this substitution. In Luke 16, the rich man is in torment in Hades while Lazarus rests in "Abraham's bosom," suggesting a subdivided realm rather than the neutral Sheol of the older texts.

Verdict: a Greek concept grafted onto a Hebrew one by translation
Gehenna
Aramaic / Greek · from a real place, the Valley of Hinnom

Named for an actual valley outside Jerusalem, historically the site of child sacrifice to Molech, condemned repeatedly by the biblical prophets and desecrated by King Josiah's reforms around 622 BCE specifically to prevent further use.

By the time of Jesus, the valley's horrific history had turned its name into shorthand for divine judgment. Jesus uses Gehenna roughly a dozen times in the Synoptic Gospels, more than any other term for post-mortem punishment. The Mishnah describes a maximum twelve-month purifying punishment there, not eternal torment for most people.

Verdict: a real place, weaponized into a metaphor, later read back as eternal
Tartarus
Greek mythology · New Testament hapax legomenon

In Greek myth, the deepest pit beneath even Hades, reserved for the worst offenders, most famously the Titans. The word appears exactly once in the entire Bible, 2 Peter 2:4, and only as a verb, "cast into Tartarus."

It is used there specifically for rebellious angels, not human beings, echoing the earlier Jewish text 1 Enoch, which had already borrowed the term for the same purpose. This is about as direct a case of wholesale mythological borrowing as exists anywhere in the New Testament.

Verdict: lifted straight from Greek mythology, for angels only
The Full Timeline

How a neutral Hebrew word for "the grave" became the architecture of eternal conscious torment, in roughly two thousand years and a dozen distinct historical moves.

Pre-exilic

Sheol, the Neutral Grave

Early Hebrew texts describe Sheol as the common, morally neutral destination of the dead. No judgment, no reward, no punishment, just an end to activity and consciousness.

8th–7th c. BCE

The Valley of Hinnom's Real History

Kings Ahaz and Manasseh are recorded sacrificing children to Molech in the Valley of Hinnom, just outside Jerusalem's walls. The prophet Jeremiah condemns the practice in the strongest terms, renaming it "the Valley of Slaughter."

622 BCE

Josiah Desecrates the Valley

King Josiah's religious reforms formally defile the Topheth in the Valley of Hinnom specifically to make it permanently unusable for sacrifice, according to 2 Kings 23:10. The valley's association with horror is now sealed.

538–331 BCE
Genuinely debated

Persian Rule and Zoroastrian Contact

Under Persian control after the Babylonian exile, Judean thought develops new interest in angels, cosmic dualism, resurrection, and final judgment, ideas Zoroastrianism had held for centuries. Whether this is direct influence or independent, parallel development is a live scholarly argument, not settled consensus, and Bart Ehrman himself has revised his view on it over time.

c. 300s–100s BCE

The Septuagint Substitutes Hades for Sheol

Translating the Hebrew Bible into Greek, scholars render Sheol as Hades throughout, importing Greek mythological baggage into what had been a distinct Hebrew concept. This single translation choice reshapes the entire vocabulary that follows.

c. 300–100 BCE

1 Enoch Divides the Dead

This influential Jewish text, not part of the Hebrew Bible but widely read in the period, describes four separate chambers for the dead based on moral character, the first real step toward a punitive afterlife. It also borrows the Greek term Tartarus, chapter 20, to describe the prison of fallen angels, a full New Testament generation before 2 Peter uses the same word.

c. 165 BCE

Daniel Introduces Resurrection and Judgment

Daniel 12:2, one of the latest-written books of the Hebrew Bible, is the clearest Old Testament statement of bodily resurrection and post-mortem separation of the righteous and wicked, a genuine turning point after centuries of Sheol's neutrality.

1st c. CE

The New Testament Inherits All Four Words

Jesus uses Gehenna roughly a dozen times in the Synoptics. Hades appears in Luke's parable of the rich man and Lazarus. Revelation introduces the "lake of fire" as yet another image. And 2 Peter 2:4 borrows Tartarus for fallen angels specifically. Four distinct vocabularies, never fully reconciled into one system within the text itself.

c. 197 CE

Tertullian's Spectacle

In De Spectaculis, Tertullian writes that one of the pleasures awaiting the faithful in heaven will be watching the torment of the damned, kings, philosophers, and persecutors alike, burning below. Eternal conscious torment is now not just doctrine but entertainment.

c. 230 CE

Origen Proposes Universal Restoration

The theologian Origen teaches apokatastasis, the eventual reconciliation of all rational beings, including the devil himself, to God, after a purifying but ultimately temporary punishment. This view is held by other major early theologians too, including Gregory of Nyssa.

c. 400s CE

Augustine Codifies Eternal Conscious Torment

In The City of God, Augustine argues forcefully against Origen's universalism and for the eternal, unending nature of hell's punishment. His view becomes the dominant position across Western Christianity for the next fifteen hundred years.

543 & 553 CE
Historically disputed

Origen's View Is Condemned, Sort Of

A regional council at Constantinople in 543 condemns a list of Origenist positions including universal restoration. Whether the wider Fifth Ecumenical Council in 553 formally ratified this condemnation, or whether the relevant anathemas were added to the record afterward, is a genuine, ongoing dispute among historians of the period, not a settled fact either side likes to admit.

c. 1200 CE
Popular myth, corrected

The Burning Garbage Dump That Wasn't

The claim that Gehenna was a smoldering trash heap outside Jerusalem in Jesus's day, repeated in countless sermons and study Bibles, traces to a single medieval rabbi, David Kimhi, writing thirteen centuries after the fact. No ancient source, textual or archaeological, supports it. Bart Ehrman and multiple Biblical Archaeologist studies have confirmed the claim has no earlier foundation.

1308–1321 CE

Dante Writes the Inferno

Dante Alighieri's poem organizes hell into nine concentric circles, each punishing a specific category of sin with a punishment that mirrors it, a device called contrapasso. The structure draws heavily on Aristotle's ethics and Greco-Roman mythology (Minos, the judge of the Greek underworld, appears as hell's gatekeeper), far more than on any single biblical passage. This becomes the single most influential image of hell in Western culture.

16th c. CE

The Reformers Hold the Line

Calvin and Luther both retain eternal conscious torment as orthodox doctrine, and both explicitly oppose apokatastasis by name. The Augustinian settlement survives the single biggest rupture in Western Christian history intact.

19th–21st c.

The Settlement Starts to Crack

Conditional immortality, the view that the unsaved are ultimately destroyed rather than tormented forever, gains ground among some Protestant groups including Seventh-day Adventists and Jehovah's Witnesses. Evangelical universalism resurfaces in the mainstream too, most visibly with Rob Bell's 2011 book Love Wins, which reopened a debate Augustine had supposedly closed sixteen centuries earlier.

Dante's Fingerprints

A striking amount of what people assume is "biblical" about hell was invented, or at minimum systematized, by one fourteenth-century poet working from Aristotle and Greek mythology as much as scripture.

What Dante Added

Nine concentric circles sorted by specific sin. Contrapasso, punishment that poetically mirrors the crime, lustful souls buffeted by endless wind, the greedy pushing weights against each other forever. Minos, borrowed directly from Greek mythology, as hell's judge, assigning souls to their circle.

What the Text Actually Says

The Gospels describe "outer darkness" and "weeping and gnashing of teeth," with no circles, no geography, and no Aristotelian sorting mechanism. The idea of degrees of punishment exists in scattered NT verses (Luke 10:14, Matthew 10:15), but nothing resembling Dante's structured architecture.

Parallel Underworlds

Sheol wasn't unique. Most of the ancient Near East and Mediterranean world had some version of an underworld before anyone had a version of punishment.

Mesopotamian Irkalla

A dark, dusty "land of no return" where the dead eat clay and drink dirty water, regardless of how they lived. As grim and morally neutral as early Sheol, and likely part of the same broader cultural inheritance.

Egyptian Duat

Unlike Mesopotamia and early Israel, Egypt developed individual moral judgment early: the heart weighed against the feather of Ma'at, with the unworthy devoured by Ammit rather than merely existing in shadow.

Greek Hades & Tartarus

Covered above, Hades as the general realm of the dead, Tartarus as its deepest, most punitive pit, reserved mythologically for the Titans and directly borrowed into Jewish and Christian texts for fallen angels.

Zoroastrian Chinvat Bridge

Souls cross a bridge that widens for the righteous and narrows to a blade's edge for the wicked, who fall into a house of torment below. The possible influence of this on Second Temple Jewish eschatology is genuinely contested, see the timeline above, not a settled borrowing.

None of this requires that hell is false, only that it isn't simple. Four words, three languages, a real valley with a horrifying history, a borrowed Greek prison for angels, a fifth-century theological argument that may or may not have been formally settled, and a medieval Italian poet who did more to furnish popular imagination than any single verse of the text everyone assumes it came from.
