Before Monotheism

El and Asherah: The God Before the God

Most Christians assume monotheism is where the story starts, one God, always, from Genesis 1 onward. The archaeology and the text itself tell a slower story. The god of Israel began as one member of a much larger Canaanite pantheon, with a father, a mother, and siblings, and only became "the only God" gradually, over roughly six hundred years, through a process scholars can trace in real inscriptions and real manuscript variants.

The Pantheon Before the Bible

In 1929, a farmer's plow near the Syrian coast struck the roof of an ancient tomb at Ras Shamra, ancient Ugarit. The tablets found there, dating to roughly 1400–1200 BCE, describe an entire Canaanite pantheon in detail the Bible itself never gives, because the Bible was written by the people trying to replace it.

El

Aged, wise father-god, head of the pantheon, dwelling at the source of the cosmic rivers. Title: "Father of years."

Asherah / Athirat

El's consort, "mother of the seventy gods," also called "the one who walks in the sea." Cult symbol: a sacred tree or pole.

Baal

Storm and fertility god, becomes the pantheon's practical warrior-king by defeating Yam

Yam

Sea and chaos god, El's initial choice for king, later deposed by Baal

Mot

God of death and the underworld, Baal's other great rival

Anat

War goddess, Baal's sister and fiercest ally

Shapash

Sun goddess, "torch of the gods," a mediating figure in the myths

Yahweh

One of the "sons of El" in the oldest layer of the tradition, later absorbs El's own identity entirely

Asherah's standard epithet, "mother of the seventy gods," is echoed in the Hebrew Bible's own framing of seventy nations descended from Noah (Genesis 10), and possibly in Deuteronomy 32:8's "sons of God" allotting the nations, a detail explored below.
Yahweh Enters the Pantheon

This isn't inference from silence. It's preserved directly in the manuscript tradition of the Hebrew Bible itself, in a passage later scribes clearly found uncomfortable enough to quietly rewrite.

Strong — direct manuscript evidence

Deuteronomy 32:8-9 describes the High God, El Elyon, dividing up the nations of the earth among his divine sons, with Yahweh receiving Israel as his particular portion. The oldest surviving versions and the version everyone reads today say two different things.

Dead Sea Scrolls & Septuagint (older)

"...he set the boundaries of the peoples according to the number of the sons of God. For Yahweh's portion was his people, Jacob his allotted share."

Masoretic Text (later, standard)

"...he set the boundaries of the peoples according to the number of the sons of Israel. For the LORD's portion is his people, Jacob his allotted inheritance."

What Changed, and Why

Textual critic Emanuel Tov calls the Masoretic reading an "anti-polytheistic alteration." The older text places El Elyon above Yahweh, assigning him Israel the way a king assigns provinces to his sons. A later scribe, uncomfortable with a Yahweh who is subordinate to another god, quietly swapped "sons of God" for "sons of Israel," erasing the hierarchy in five letters.

Over subsequent centuries, Yahweh absorbs El's own titles directly, El Shaddai, El Elyon, and eventually simply El, until the two are treated as one and the same god. Scholars including Francesca Stavrakopoulou call this process "pantheon reduction," the slow contraction of many gods into one, rather than monotheism being the starting point.

Asherah, Yahweh's Consort
Genuinely debated

In 1975 and 1976, inscriptions were found at two sites in the Kingdom of Judah, Kuntillet Ajrud in the northern Sinai and Khirbet el-Qom near Hebron, both dated to the 8th century BCE. A blessing formula recurs across them: "by Yahweh... and by his Asherah."

The Case for a Goddess

Asherah's name here carries a possessive suffix, "his Asherah," a construction that in Hebrew grammar normally attaches to a person, not an abstract object. Combined with over a thousand female pillar figurines recovered from the same period, many scholars read this as evidence that Yahweh was worshipped alongside a consort goddess well into the Israelite monarchy.

The Case for a Cult Object

Other scholars argue "asherah" here refers to a wooden cultic pole or symbol associated with the goddess's worship, not the deity herself, since Hebrew personal names don't usually take possessive suffixes this way either. The debate remains genuinely unresolved in the field, not a settled question either side likes to admit.

What isn't disputed: 2 Kings 23 records King Josiah physically removing vessels made for Baal and Asherah, and an Asherah pole itself, from inside the Jerusalem Temple, meaning some form of her worship had been happening in Yahweh's own house, not at some rival shrine down the road.

The Divine Council

Psalm 82 preserves, almost intact, a scene straight out of the Ugaritic pantheon: a high god presiding over a council of other gods and passing judgment on them.

Psalm 82:1, 6

"God has taken his place in the divine council; in the midst of the gods he holds judgment... I said, 'You are gods, sons of the Most High, all of you.'"

Read plainly, this is Yahweh presiding over other divine beings, not human judges, a reading most modern critical scholars accept, though later Jewish and Christian tradition reinterpreted the "gods" here as either angels or human rulers to soften the polytheism.

Exodus 15:11

"Who is like you among the gods, O Yahweh?"

This is henotheism stated outright, worshipping one god as supreme while explicitly acknowledging that other gods exist. It's an earlier, more honest stage of the theology than the flat monotheism most modern readers assume was always there.

From Many Gods to One

The shift wasn't a single event. It was a centuries-long argument, with a real turning point.

c. 1200–1000 BCE

Yahweh, One God Among Many

Early Israelite religion functions as one variant of the broader Canaanite religious world, with Yahweh worshipped as Israel's particular patron god within a wider, acknowledged pantheon.

c. 900–700 BCE

Monolatry Takes Hold

Prophets increasingly insist Yahweh alone should be worshipped by Israel, without yet denying other gods exist for other nations. This is monolatry, not monotheism, worship one, but don't necessarily claim the others are unreal.

622 BCE

Josiah's Purge

King Josiah's reforms centralize worship in Jerusalem and physically remove Asherah's cult objects from the Temple itself, part of a broader campaign to strip out the older, plural religious landscape.

c. 540 BCE
Debated among scholars

Second Isaiah's Turn

Writing during the Babylonian exile, the author of Isaiah 40-55 declares "I am the LORD, and there is no other; besides me there is no god" (45:5), the first genuinely unambiguous denial that other gods exist anywhere in the Hebrew Bible. Some scholars caution that even this text still describes a heavenly host and echoes the older Yahweh-versus-sea-dragon combat myth, so how "radical" the monotheism really is stays a live argument, not a clean line.

None of this is a secret held back from believers, it's mainstream biblical scholarship, taught in seminaries and stated plainly in academic study Bibles. It rarely reaches the pews, not because it's controversial among scholars, but because the popular version of the story, one God, always, needs the earlier chapters left out to stay simple. The god who became "the only God" had a father, a mother, and a family, and the text remembers them, if you read closely enough to notice.
