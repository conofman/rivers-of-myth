Rivers of Myth: Mesopotamia downstream

Long before Genesis or Hesiod were written down, the scribes of Sumer and Akkad were already recording floods, creations, and gods who crossed into the underworld. This chart traces the strongest documented lines from those cuneiform originals into the Hebrew Bible and Greek mythology, and marks where later popular claims (Christ and the "dying-and-rising god," in particular) reach further than the primary texts support.

Read left to right: wellspring, transmission, and the two downstream traditions. Solid lines mark parallels with strong textual or linguistic evidence. Dashed lines mark real but contested or structurally looser connections.

WellspringSumerian & Akkadian, c. 2100–1200 BCE
Sumerian / Akkadianc. 2100–1200 BCE

Eridu Genesis, Atrahasis & Gilgamesh's Flood

A god warns one man of a coming flood, he builds a boat, loads animals aboard, sends out birds to find dry land, and offers sacrifice afterward. The template both later flood myths follow almost beat for beat.

Babylonianc. 1750–1100 BCE

Enuma Elish

Marduk orders the cosmos out of Tiamat, the primordial salt-water chaos. A watery formlessness organized into a structured world by a ruling god.

Sumerianc. 1900 BCE

Dumuzid & the Descent of Inanna

A shepherd-consort god passes into the underworld and returns for part of each year, tied to the seasonal death and revival of vegetation.

Akkadianc. 1400 BCE

Adapa and the South Wind

A wise man is offered the bread and water of immortality before the gods and, misled, refuses them. Immortality lost through a choice at a divine table.

TransmissionAnatolia & the Levant, c. 1400–700 BCE
Hurrian–Hittitec. 1400–1200 BCE

The Kumarbi Cycle

A generational succession of sky-gods, each overthrowing the father before him, passed down through Anatolia toward the Aegean.

Northwest Semiticc. 1200–800 BCE

The Title "Adon" (Lord)

A Semitic honorific for a consort-god, carried into the Greek world and eventually adopted as a proper name.

Greek philosophyc. 300 BCE–1st c. CE

Stoic & Platonic Logos

An independent Greek philosophical line, not Mesopotamian: the ordering rational principle of the cosmos, later grafted onto Hebrew wisdom tradition.

Greek MythArchaic–Classical, c. 750–100 BCE
Greekrecorded c. 1st c. BCE

Deucalion & Pyrrha

Zeus floods a corrupted earth; a righteous couple survives by boat and repopulates the world. Independent descent from the same Near Eastern flood tradition as Genesis, not a copy of it.

Greekc. 700 BCE

Hesiod's Theogony

Uranus is overthrown by Cronus, Cronus by Zeus. The succession pattern maps closely onto the Kumarbi cycle's line of deposed sky-fathers.

Greekarchaic–classical

Adonis

A mortal beloved of Aphrodite who dies young and is mourned each year. The name and the seasonal death-and-mourning structure both trace to Dumuzid by way of the Semitic title "adon."

Hebrew BibleComposed / compiled c. 6th c. BCE
Hebrewc. 6th c. BCE

Genesis 6–9, Noah's Flood

Same sequence as the Mesopotamian flood texts: divine warning, an ark, paired animals, birds sent to test for land, sacrifice on landing. Likely transmitted during or after the Babylonian exile.

Hebrewc. 6th c. BCE

Genesis 1, Creation from the Deep

The Hebrew tehom, the primordial deep, is linguistically related to Tiamat. Formless waters are separated and ordered into a structured cosmos by a single ruling god.

Hebrewc. 6th c. BCE

Genesis 2–3, Eden's Forbidden Fruit

Adapa is offered immortality and refuses it; Eden's couple is forbidden fruit and take it anyway. A real thematic echo, but inverted, not a direct copy.

Egyptianindependent, c. 2400 BCE+

Osiris

Often grouped with Dumuzid and Adonis as a "dying-and-rising god." Osiris in fact becomes lord of the dead rather than returning among the living, and the Egyptian tradition is old enough to be independent rather than borrowed.

Popular grouping overstates the parallel
Christian, NTc. 90–110 CE

John 1:1, "In the beginning was the Word"

Logos is imported directly from Greek philosophy, not from Mesopotamian myth. A case of Greek metaphysics grafted onto Hebrew wisdom writing.

Popular claimwidely circulated

Resurrection & the "Dying-and-Rising God"

The category itself has been challenged by scholars, notably Jonathan Z. Smith, as a template imposed on myths that don't actually share a resurrection structure in their original texts.

Category disputed by comparative religion scholarship

Reading this chart

This is a simplified map, not a complete stemma. Oral tradition moved through trade routes, migration, and conquest for centuries before any of these texts were fixed in writing, so "borrowing" here means the closest documented ancestor, not a single traceable act of copying. Where a popular claim outruns what the primary sources say, that's marked rather than smoothed over.
