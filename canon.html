How the Book Got Made

The Bible wasn't handed down complete. It was assembled over four centuries by specific people making specific decisions, some theological, some political, some as blunt as "which texts does everyone already agree on." This is the actual timeline, what got left out along the way, and a few translation choices that changed what the text says.

The Timeline
c. 50–60s CE

Paul's Letters Come First

Paul's authentic letters, Romans, 1 Corinthians, Galatians, and others, are the earliest Christian writings that survive, written years before any Gospel. The Jesus movement existed for a generation as letters and oral tradition, not a book.

c. 70–100 CE

The Four Gospels Are Written

Mark is generally dated first, around 70 CE, near the fall of the Jerusalem Temple. Matthew and Luke follow around 80–90 CE, both drawing on Mark and a hypothesized shared sayings-source scholars call Q. John comes last, around 90–100 CE, with noticeably different theology and chronology from the other three.

c. 100–150 CE

Many Gospels, No Fixed List

Dozens of gospels, acts, and revelations circulate among different Christian communities, including the Gospel of Thomas, the Gospel of Peter, and the Infancy Gospel of Thomas. There is no single authoritative New Testament yet, just a patchwork of texts read in different regions.

c. 144 CE

Marcion Forces the Issue

Marcion of Sinope proposes the first fixed Christian canon: an edited Gospel of Luke and ten Pauline letters, and nothing else, rejecting the entire Hebrew Bible as the work of a lesser god. The wider church declares him a heretic, but his hard line pushes everyone else to start defining their own.

c. 170 CE

The Muratorian Fragment

The earliest surviving list that resembles the eventual New Testament, naming most of the familiar books while explicitly flagging others as disputed, the Apocalypse of Peter among them, with the note that "some do not want it read in church."

c. 180 CE

Irenaeus Argues for Exactly Four

Irenaeus of Lyon insists there must be exactly four gospels, no more and no fewer, reasoning from the four corners of the earth and the four winds. A theological argument, not a historical or evidentiary one.

325 CE
Popular myth, corrected

Nicaea (Not About the Canon)

Despite what circulates online, the Council of Nicaea did not vote on which books belong in the Bible. It was convened to settle the Arian controversy, the argument over whether Christ was of the same substance as God the Father, and produced the Nicene Creed. Canon formation was never on its agenda.

367 CE

Athanasius Names the 27

In his Easter letter, Bishop Athanasius of Alexandria becomes the first person on record to list exactly the 27 books that make up today's New Testament, and only those 27, nothing more and nothing less.

393 & 397 CE

Regional Councils Ratify It

The Councils of Hippo and Carthage, regional North African church councils, formally endorse Athanasius's list for the Western church, though it takes centuries more before it's treated as fully settled everywhere.

1546 CE

Trent Locks the Old Testament

In direct response to the Protestant Reformation, the Catholic Council of Trent formally dogmatizes its Old Testament canon, including the deuterocanonical books, Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, Sirach, Baruch, 1 and 2 Maccabees, that Protestant Bibles leave out.

16th c.

Luther Draws a Different Line

Martin Luther moves those same deuterocanonical books into a separate Apocrypha section, useful for reading but not authoritative for doctrine, and privately doubted James, Hebrews, Jude, and Revelation, though he kept all 27 New Testament books in his translation.

What Got Left Out

Gospel of Thomas

A "sayings gospel," 114 numbered lines attributed to Jesus with no narrative, no crucifixion, no resurrection. Associated with early Christian mysticism.

Gospel of Judas

Rediscovered in the 20th century. Portrays Judas not as a betrayer but as the one disciple who truly understood Jesus, acting at his request.

Gospel of Mary

Centers Mary Magdalene as a leading disciple and visionary, in open tension with the other apostles over her authority to teach.

Infancy Gospel of Thomas

Stories of a child Jesus performing miracles, some startling and violent. Popular reading, never seriously considered for the canon.

Shepherd of Hermas

Widely read and even bound into some early manuscript collections, including Codex Sinaiticus, but ultimately left out of the final list.

Didache

An early church training manual on ethics and ritual, influential enough that it came close to inclusion in some regional canons.

Translation Shifts That Mattered

Isaiah 7:14

"Young Woman" Becomes "Virgin"

The Hebrew word almah means "young woman," with no necessary implication of virginity, that's a separate Hebrew word, betulah. The Greek Septuagint translates almah as parthenos, "virgin," and Matthew's Gospel cites that Greek version, not the Hebrew, to frame Jesus's birth as a fulfillment of prophecy.

Psalm 22:16

"Like a Lion" or "They Pierced"

The Masoretic Hebrew text reads "like a lion, my hands and feet," while the Dead Sea Scrolls and the Greek Septuagint preserve a different reading, "they pierced my hands and feet." Which version is original has been debated for centuries, and the pierced reading became central to later crucifixion-as-prophecy arguments.

Multiple Words, One English Term

"Hell" as a Catch-All

English Bibles translate at least four distinct underlying words, Sheol, Hades, Gehenna, and Tartarus, as a single word, hell. Each had its own separate meaning in its original context. Flattening them into one English term erases centuries of theological development, and is its own chart on this site.

None of this requires bad faith on anyone's part to explain. Canons form the way most institutional documents form, through argument, regional habit, and eventually consensus, not a single moment of decree. The Nicaea myth is worth killing specifically because it's the most repeated claim about this whole process and the least accurate one.
